Somewhere in your dependency tree right now is a package maintained by one person, in their spare time, for free. Maybe it's a date-formatting utility, maybe it's a critical piece of your build tooling. Either way, there's a good chance you've never thought about who keeps it running.

## The invisible labor

Studies of major package registries keep finding the same thing: a huge share of widely-used open source software is maintained by a tiny number of people, often unpaid, often burning out. Maintaining a popular project isn't just writing code — it's triaging issues, reviewing pull requests from strangers, responding to security reports, and fielding demands from companies who depend on the software but have never contributed anything back.

## Why this matters to you

If your production stack depends on volunteer-maintained software, you are already exposed to a kind of risk that doesn't show up on any dashboard: what happens when that person stops. Projects have been abandoned overnight. Some have been quietly handed off to bad actors who slipped malicious code into a routine-looking update, precisely because no one was watching closely enough.

## What actually helps

- **File better issues.** A clear, minimal reproduction saves a maintainer hours compared to "it doesn't work."
- **Sponsor projects you depend on**, even a small amount — many maintainers report that modest, recurring sponsorship matters more than one large one-time gift.
- **Contribute fixes upstream** instead of patching around bugs locally.
- **Say thank you.** It sounds small, but maintainers consistently mention that a genuine note of appreciation is rare enough to be memorable.

## A shift worth making

None of this requires a grand gesture. It requires treating the free software your company runs on as something that has real people behind it, with real limits — and building that awareness into how your team works, not just how it complains.
